If you complete logistics-sandbox and want to turn this into something serious, I would recommend building a Logistics Intelligence & Optimization Platform on top of it.

Not another simulation project. Not simply a fleet-management dashboard.

Something that helps actual businesses answer:

How should we operate our logistics network, what will happen if conditions change, and which decisions will produce the best results?

That is a much more ambitious and useful direction.

The platform I would build
LONG-TERM PRODUCT VISION

Logistics Intelligence Platform
A logistics control tower combining real operations, simulation, optimization, and decision support.

Imagine a delivery company uploads its orders, fleet, depots, and historical delivery records.

Your platform builds a model of its operations and allows the manager to experiment with decisions before committing to them.

For example:

What happens if we add 10 delivery vehicles?

Would opening another depot reduce costs?

Which dispatch strategy is best for our deliveries?

What happens if a major road becomes unavailable?

Can we deliver the same number of orders with fewer vehicles?

How would changing delivery time windows affect performance?

The system runs those scenarios, compares the results, and provides evidence for a recommendation.

The roadmap I'd follow
Phase 1 — Finish Logistics Sandbox

Make simulation deterministic, replayable, measurable, and genuinely connected to osm-pathfinder.

Success: the same scenario can be reproduced and compared across algorithms.

Phase 2 — Build a serious optimization engine

Add capacitated VRP, delivery time windows, driver scheduling, multi-stop optimization, and optimization benchmarks.

Start with Google OR-Tools as a baseline rather than building every optimizer from scratch. It already supports these core vehicle-routing constraints. 

developers.google.com
+1

Phase 3 — Build the decision platform

Let an operator import a real delivery dataset, create alternative scenarios, run comparisons, and see potential cost/time savings with assumptions clearly exposed.

This is the first version I would consider testing with a real business.

Phase 4 — Connect real operations

Integrate actual orders, vehicle tracking, driver data, and delivery outcomes. Compare predictions with reality to calibrate the models.

Only at this stage would I seriously describe the system as an operational digital twin, rather than simply a simulation.

Phase 5 — Turn it into a hosted business product

Add organizations, RBAC, audit logs, billing, API integrations, monitoring, backups, tenant isolation, and production deployment.

This is where cloud infrastructure becomes a necessity rather than another technical experiment.

A more plausible niche worth validating is:

An accessible logistics decision laboratory for smaller delivery and distribution businesses that cannot justify complex enterprise simulation software.

For a Cambodia-focused first pilot, you could even start with a CSV import instead of requiring expensive real-time infrastructure.

Actual results must be reported with model assumptions, uncertainty, and validation against real delivery records—not presented as guaranteed savings.
This is something a logistics manager could potentially use to make a decision, rather than just admire.

What I would prioritize most
Before spending months building a complete platform, I would try to answer one question:

Can your system take a company's real historical delivery records and identify a change that is measurably better than their current process?

That's your first serious product milestone.

If it can, you've moved beyond proving that your code works. You're testing whether your technology delivers business value.

And one last thing: I wouldn't generalize it into a universal simulation platform yet. You already have plenty of components; the next challenge is depth, accuracy, and usefulness.

My recommendation: Finish logistics-sandbox → build an optimization and scenario-comparison layer → test it with real logistics data → then decide whether to turn it into a commercial platform.

That path could turn the experiments from your DWH and math assignments into the technical foundation of an actual business.
