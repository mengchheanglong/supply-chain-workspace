Enterprise Logistics Intelligence Platform
Research and architecture assessment · October 8, 2026
After inspecting your three GitHub repositories, I recommend developing them into an enterprise distribution intelligence, optimization, and digital-twin platform.
The strongest direction is not to create another generic logistics-management application. It is to build a system that helps major distributors, manufacturers, retailers, and third-party logistics providers understand their distribution networks, optimize operations, simulate disruptions, and evaluate decisions before executing them.
There is an important distinction:
- Your current product: A visual logistics simulator connected to routing and experimental data infrastructure.
- Your target product: An enterprise decision platform that integrates with existing business systems, uses real operational data, and produces defensible recommendations.
- Your competitive challenge: Established enterprise vendors already offer significant parts of this vision. You will need to prove a specific advantage, not simply combine more technologies.

Your logistics repository has already progressed beyond the initial specification. It includes a multi-stop tour solver, capacity-aware dispatch logic, VRPTW-related scenario tests, fleet analytics, operator incidents, an upstream e-commerce bridge, and Cassandra/MongoDB adapters.
Those are useful foundations. However, the current VRP solver is primarily a nearest-neighbor, capacity-constrained heuristic, rather than a general industrial vehicle-routing optimizer with verified constraint handling.
There are also some immediate architectural concerns in the source:
- The routing client falls back to straight-line routes when the routing service fails. This is acceptable for a clearly labeled demo mode, but must not silently contaminate enterprise planning results.
- Simulation code still uses Math.random() and wall-clock calls in some paths, despite the deterministic-replay objective.
- The in-memory event bus and asynchronous persistence calls do not yet provide durable, replayable, exactly-once business effects.
- The e-commerce integration can create orders, update fulfillment status, and adjust inventory. Those are dangerous operations to connect directly to hypothetical simulation scenarios.
- The Cassandra adapter keeps a local fallback buffer and sends data through the marketplace API; that does not establish that Cassandra itself has been exercised or scaled.
- The e-commerce project's telemetry service contains a static rider dataset, while its actual Cassandra persistence design is largely separate from that API response.
My first major architectural decision would be to separate simulation from production operations completely. A hypothetical depot closure must never change the inventory or real customer orders in a connected business system.
This audit is based on the latest accessible GitHub source. I have not independently deployed the three repositories or verified their runtime performance.

5. How to make scenario results scientifically defensible
Your current sandbox has a good concept: run the same scenario against different algorithms.
For enterprise planning, extend it into a reproducible experiment contract:
{
  "snapshot_id": "distribution_2026_10_v3",
  "scenario_id": "depot_b_closure",
  "simulation_seed": 82134,
  "road_graph_version": "kh_2026_10",
  "cost_model_version": "cost_v3",
  "optimizer": "ortools",
  "objective": "total_distribution_cost",
  "horizon_hours": 24
}


Every run needs its own immutable data snapshot, simulation seed, routing graph version, cost assumptions and solver configuration.
Then the comparison becomes meaningful.
SCENARIO A · BASELINE
Current operations
Existing delivery assignments and fleet configuration.

Evaluate
Actual or reconstructed distribution cost, OTIF, fleet utilization and exceptions.


SCENARIO B · ALTERNATIVE
Optimized operations
Same demand, constraints and road conditions, with a changed strategy.

Evaluate
Feasibility, projected cost difference, service-level impact and uncertainty.



Crucially, an optimizer finding a lower theoretical route cost does not prove a real business will achieve those savings. You first have to reconstruct historical operations accurately and compare predictions against observed outcomes.

8. Business validation: the part that determines whether this becomes a real company
Building an impressive product and proving that large businesses need it are two different achievements.
My proposed early customer-discovery target is 15–25 conversations with operations planners, logistics managers, IT integration teams, and executives at regional distribution businesses.
The important questions are not whether they think a simulation looks interesting. They are whether they have expensive decisions that their current tools cannot answer confidently, whether their necessary data is available, and what evidence would justify changing their operations.
Your first commercial pilot should produce a concrete deliverable: a historical distribution-network assessment comparing the current operational plan with two feasible alternatives, including financial assumptions, data-quality limitations, and an implementation recommendation.
That is a service an enterprise can evaluate. It also creates real feedback for the software.
